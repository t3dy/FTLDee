# Exposition — narration, sector intros, the departure, epilogues

Narrator copy. The narrator speaks directly; real people appear only in reported speech.
Each passage carries its status. Where a passage sits on a counterfactual branch, the
status line is shown on screen, not just stored in data.

**Page numbers** follow `docs/CITATIONS.md` (revised 2026-10-04 after
`review/WRITING_READ1.md`): Parry, Harkness, Szőnyi and Whitby are cited by **printed**
page; Fenton, Fell Smith, Sherman, Håkansson and Clucas by the PDF page of the DeeChunks
copy, marked "(DeeChunks p.)" where it matters.

## 1. Opening narration (new game, before the Prologue)

> Mortlake, on the Thames above London, about 1580.
>
> John Dee is past fifty. He has lectured on Euclid in Paris, brought globes back from
> Louvain, written the preface to the first English Euclid and a book of symbols dedicated
> to an emperor. When the Queen was crowned he cast a horoscope for the day the Council had
> already chosen. He has explained comets to her. He has never been given the post he
> thinks his learning deserves.
>
> What he has instead is a house. In it is the largest private library in England, more
> than four thousand volumes, over seven hundred of them manuscripts, many rescued from the
> monastic libraries scattered at the Dissolution. Dee called them his "hardly gotten
> moniments". There are laboratories, instruments, a wife who keeps the accounts, and a
> household of servants, assistants and, soon, scryers.
>
> This is a game about running that house as an institution nobody would fund: what to
> build, whom to trust, which books to carry, and what to do when the political weather
> changes before the work is finished.

*Status: documented. Sources: Parry 14–37 (Louvain, Paris, the Euclid); Parry 49 (the
Council chose 15 January; Dee's election horoscope interpreted the appointed day);
Whitby 19–24 (globes, library, laboratories); Håkansson 12–14, DeeChunks p. (the size of
the library and the quotation).*

Then a single line under the title card, before play:

> Every card in this game says whether it happened. When it did not, the game tells you.

## 2. The Prologue, 1555 (remembered)

Shown before the first Prologue encounter (`prologue_1555`).

> Before Mortlake, before the library, there was 1555.
>
> Queen Mary is thought to be with child. Her half-sister Elizabeth is under guard at
> Woodstock. A young mathematician is asked to cast figures for the princess, for the
> Queen and for the King. To cast them is to ask when a queen will die.
>
> What happened next shaped everything Dee did afterwards: the arrest, the written
> questions, and the bishop's household where he ended up. The game lets you choose
> differently. It tells you when you have.

*Status: documented (Parry 31–39). The encounters mark the choices contrary to the record.*

## 3. England sector intro (sector card)

> **England, 1580–1583. Southern England and the Thames.**
>
> From Mortlake the roads go to the Queen at Richmond, Hampton Court and Windsor, to
> Walsingham at Barn Elms, to the booksellers of Paul's Churchyard and the printers at
> Aldersgate, the pilots at Deptford and Muscovy House, and the colleges at Oxford. Every
> journey costs days, and the days are counted.
>
> The court wants useful learning that can be kept discreet, and pays in promises. The
> scholars want books and letters. Burghley wants service that costs little. Nobody wants
> to pay for the library.
>
> In 150 days a Polish nobleman will ask Dee to leave England. What Dee can take, and what
> he can say yes to, depends on what he does before then.

*Status: documented frame; the 180-day sector compresses about three years.*

## 4. The departure and the packing scene

Fires after the Continental Question if the player chooses to leave.

### 4a. Before packing

> Łaski's offer is accepted. The household has days, not weeks.
>
> Most of the library cannot go. It is too large, and what goes must travel by water to
> the ships beyond Gravesend, across the North Sea and then overland. The instruments that
> are fixed in the house stay with the house. The rest is a question of slots.
>
> Choose what goes in the satchel. Everything else stays at Mortlake.

*Status: documented. Dee left with Łaski on 21 September 1583 (Parry 170–171; Whitby
27–29).*

### 4b. On confirming the satchel

> The satchel is closed. {book} goes in last, on top.

## 5. The Road East sector intro (sector card)

> **The Road East, 1583–1584.**
>
> Two ships beyond Gravesend; landfall at Brill; a Dutch hoy; the Baltic towns; Stettin on
> Christmas morning; Posen; Łaski's own estate at Lask, mortgaged; and Kraków, reached on
> 13 March 1584. Nobody in the household has licence to leave England.
>
> There is no house now, only what the household carries. Rooms can be set up to their
> first level at a halt and no further. The satchel is the library.
>
> At home, the creditors are already at the door, and the people left in charge of the
> house are selling what is in it.

*Status: documented. Whitby 27–31; Fell Smith 69–70 (DeeChunks p.); Parry 170–176. The
alternative routes (Bremen, Hamburg, Danzig) are marked plausible on the map.*

## 6. Prague sector intro (sector card)

> **Prague, 1584–1586. The Emperor's city.**
>
> Dee arrived on 9 August 1584 and lodged in the house of Tadeáš Hájek, physician and
> astronomer, whose study had served for alchemical work. The house has no scriptorium and
> no room for instruments. The library is whatever came in the chest. Jane and the
> children follow from Kraków.
>
> Above the river is the Hradschin, where Rudolf II keeps his collections and his
> distance. Between the house and the castle are the Old Town, its market, and the stone
> bridge across the Vltava. Dee walked that way to his audience.
>
> Rudolf wanted the Stone more than the angels. The papal nuncio wants to know what the
> Englishman is doing in his rooms. In 120 days the city will be closed to him.

*Status: documented. Whitby 29–31 (arrival); Parry 180 (Hájek's study as an alchemical
room); Parry 182 (the walk over the bridge); Szőnyi 259; Sherman 81–85 (DeeChunks p.).
On Hájek's title: Sherman and Clulee call him the Emperor's physician; Rampling,
*Experimental Fire* 292 n. 31, calls that a common error (he treated servants at court).
The game says "physician and astronomer".*

## 8. Legend Mode (title screen and first-encounter card)

Legend Mode adds three encounters marked **[LEGEND]**: Dee signing his reports to
Walsingham with two eyes and a seven, a plot against the oaks of the Forest of Dean, and
the angelic tables as a cipher for intelligence. They are stories told about Dee long
after his death.

> **Legend Mode: the Elizabethan 007.**
> Some later writers made Dee a spy. The record does not. In this mode the legends are

## 9. Epilogues

Each epilogue has three parts: **what your career did**, **what the record has**, and a
final status line. The first part uses tokens filled from the run; the second is fixed.

### 9a. Stayed in England

> **What your Dee did.** He declined Łaski and stayed at Mortlake. The library stayed whole
> on its shelves. {house} stood at the end of the sector, and Dee's standing with the
> Queen was {faction}.
>
> **What the record has.** He did not stay. Dee left with Łaski in September 1583 and was
> abroad six years. The library and laboratories were spoiled while he was gone. He came
> back in 1589 without Kelley, defended himself before royal commissioners in 1592, and was
> made Warden of Christ's College, Manchester, in 1595: employment and exile at once. He
> died at Mortlake in December 1608 or early 1609; the parish register is lost.
>
> *Status: COUNTERFACTUAL. In the record, he left.*

**If the royal foundation was built**, insert after the first paragraph:

> The Crown endowed the house as a foundation: library, laboratories and instruments,
> with a stipend paid every ten days. This is the thing Dee asked for most of his life.
> He did not get it. Sherman's reading of his career is that the failed offices *are* the
> career.

*The 1589–1609 facts are from `research/DEE_MASTER_BIOGRAPHY.md` (Acts VI–VIII) and have
not been re-checked page by page; the reader marked them "not verified". Check before
shipping.*

### 9b. Prague

> **What your Dee did.** He took the satchel to Prague and the angels to the Emperor.
> {crew} went with him. At the end, his standing with the Continental Courts was {faction}
> and the household had {money} pounds.
>
> **What the record has.** After the nuncio's pressure Dee left Prague for Třeboň and the
> protection of Vilém Rožmberk (1586–89). There, in April 1587, the "communion of wives"
> marked the breakdown of Dee's authority in his own household, as Kelley's alchemy
> became the thing patrons valued (Parry 198–199). Dee returned to England in 1589 without
> Kelley, to a library that had been taken apart by associates and creditors, not by a mob
> (Parry 171–172). The rest of his life was spent defending his reputation and asking for
> the post he had wanted in 1580.
>
> *Status: documented.*

**If the player took a non-documented choice at the Rudolf audience**, add:

> At the Hradschin your Dee {choice summary}. The record has him rebuke the Emperor and
> urge him to trust the revelations.

### 9c. The Ottoman court (COUNTERFACTUAL)

> **What your Dee did.** He declined Łaski and took the *Monas* and the *Book of Soyga* east.
> In this career he reached the court of Murad III, a sultan who was himself a Sufi
> initiate, devoted to dream interpretation, astrology and talismans, whose reign was
> approaching the Islamic millennium. There, a man who joined mathematics, angels and
> letters into one science was a recognisable kind of adviser rather than a suspect one.
>
> **Why this is in the game.** Matthew Melvin-Koushki argues that the lore of the *Book of
> Soyga* comes from the Bunian-Bistamian magical corpus popular in Ottoman courtly circles,
> so that an eastern thread runs through Dee's first actions with spirits. He reads Ibn Turka as a
> Timurid counterpart of Dee, and Dee as an Elizabethan "Dr. Littleturk" (Melvin-Koushki 2021).
>
> **What the record has.** Dee never went east. He went to Prague and came home to
> Mortlake, where he died poor in 1608 or 1609.
>
> The game does not say whether he would have prospered there. It shows that the
> question can be asked.
>
> *Status: COUNTERFACTUAL. This did not happen.*

*Open item: the Murad III characterisation and the Bunian-Bistamian claim come from
`research/OTTOMAN_CONNECTION.md`, which cites Melvin-Koushki 2021 without pages. Find the
pages before shipping.*

### 9d. Career collapse

Shown when the run ends with Secrecy at 0 (the game's rule: "summoned for examination"),
or with Dee Destitute at the Continental Question.

> **What your Dee did.** The money ran out before the work did, or the talk about the house
> reached people who write things down. The friendships went quiet one by one: letters
> unanswered, petitions unread. {house} was still standing, but it was not an institution
> any more. It was a house with a great many books in it and not much else.
>
> **What the record has.** This is not far from how it ended. Dee spent his last years
> at Mortlake selling books to live, with his daughter Katherine keeping the house. The
> library that had been the largest in England was dispersed. Two generations later
> Méric Casaubon printed the spirit diaries (1659), and that book shaped how Dee was read
> for a long time afterwards.
>
> *Status: documented for the record; your career's timing is your own.*

Note on 9d: the Casaubon reference is *A True & Faithful Relation*, 1659 (ATTESTED in the
master biography). Katherine Dee keeping the house is from the same file and was not
verified by the reader. The 1604–05 petition to James I is left out of every epilogue on
purpose: the master biography flags it as thin in the corpus (Source Gaps, item 1).

## 10. Room exposition (one paragraph each; Codex and first-build text)

**Library.** The working collection, not a display. Dee bought, borrowed, copied and
rescued; in September 1583, before leaving, he had it catalogued. At home every book on
these shelves can be used. When he travels, only what he carries. *(Whitby 21–24;
Håkansson 12–14, DeeChunks p.)*

**Study.** The desk where the reading becomes calculation and the calculation becomes
advice. Dee's diaries record a working day spent mostly at home. It gives Focus back.

**Scriptorium.** Copying, tables and cipher. The Book of Enoch's letter tables were
written out by hand, and Dee had told Cecil about the *Steganographia* in 1563. A copy
can be sold; a cipher is easier with a second pair of eyes at the desk. *(Whitby 114–117;
Clucas, DeeChunks p.)*

**Correspondence.** Letters to Antwerp, Kraków and Prague, carried by agents and
merchants. Without them the scholarly world forgets a man who lives in a village on the
Thames. On the road, this is the letters home, and each courier is a chance for them to
be read. *(Clulee, Ambix 52.3; Szőnyi 259.)*

**Laboratory.** Furnaces, glass and minerals. Dee kept three laboratories at Mortlake,
and they were despoiled while he was abroad. *(Sherman, DeeChunks p.; Whitby 21–24,
37–39.)*

**Scrying Chamber.** The room of the actions with spirits, begun with Barnabas Saul in
1581 and continued with Edward Kelley from 1582. Its furniture was specified by the
angels themselves: the stone, the seal, the table. *(Whitby 27–29, 120–124.)*

**Instrument Room.** Mercator's globes, the astronomer's ring and staff from Louvain,
charts and declinations. Here navigation is proved, not merely argued. The globes are too
large to travel. *(Whitby 19–21.)*

**Quarters.** Family, servants, students and scryers all lived under the same roof. The
house holds as many as it holds, and no more.

**Hájek's study** (the Laboratory in Prague). Tadeáš Hájek, physician and astronomer,
lent his study, and it served for alchemical work. *(Parry 180; Sherman 81–85, DeeChunks
p.)*

**The travelling chest** (the Library on the road and in Prague). Everything that came,
and nothing that did not.

## 11. House tier exposition (Codex text)

**The house at Mortlake** (tier 1, documented). The family house by the Thames where Dee
settled after Antwerp in 1564. Big enough for the library and the work, not for the
institution he had in mind. *(Clulee, Ambix 52.3, 211–212.)*

**Mortlake enlarged** (tier 2, plausible). Adjoining rooms and outbuildings taken in.
The record has the costs of Dee's library and laboratories; it does not have this
extension. It is the building programme of a man who expects the reward to come.

**A royal foundation at Mortlake** (tier 3, COUNTERFACTUAL). The endowed institution Dee
sought and never received. In the game, it needs a grant drafted first, and then a crown
stipend pays for the library, laboratories and instruments. In the record, the petitions
without the grant.

**On the road with Łaski** (Road East, documented). Ships, a hoy, coaches. Rooms can be
set up to their first level and no further. *(Whitby 27–31.)*

**Lodging with Hájek** (Prague tier 1, documented). Rooms in a physician's house, by
courtesy. *(Szőnyi 259; Sherman 81–85, DeeChunks p.)*

**A house near the Old Town market** (Prague tier 2, documented). Dee moved there on 12
January 1585. A door of his own again. *(Whitby 31–33.)*
