## 3. Her arguments about Dee

**Dee as reader-practitioner, not only theorist.**
- Dee's alchemy has mostly been studied through the *Monas*; "Less has been said ... about Dee the
  reader of alchemical texts ... or about Dee the alchemical practitioner" (R2012a 498).
- The *Monas* "offers at best an obscured view of Dee's practical alchemical interests" (R2012a 499).
  His Mortlake collections included chemical materials and apparatus, and outbuildings where "he
  and his assistants practised alchemy" (R2012a 499). By 1583 the house had three alchemical
  workshops (R2011 143).
- The 1581 notebook (Rawlinson D.241) shows Dee "no armchair alchemist" willing "to get his hands
  dirty", pressing a slimy "quick mercury" between his fingers (R2012a 500; R2011 143).
- The *Mathematicall Praeface* (1570) omits alchemy altogether, which Rampling calls "puzzling" and
  explains by alchemy's tradition of secrecy and the danger shown by de Lannoy's fall (R2011
  143–144).

**Dee as collector of the English tradition.**
- His alchemical reading begins with the list of July 1556 (R2012a 498; R2011 143: "running to 55
  titles"). He read new printed compendia (Petreius 1541, Cyriacus Jacob 1550) alongside
  manuscripts; Sloane 2128 and 2325 were his by 1557 (EF 207).
- He was "assiduous in garnering manuscripts from colleges and former religious houses", including
  St Albans and St Augustine's, Canterbury (EF 202); great Lullian compendia (CCC 244, Mellon 12)
  came to rest "on the shelves of scholarly collectors like Dee, rather than in a monastic workroom"
  (EF 169).
- Living link to the monks: on 16 May 1561 Dee gave the former Benedictine alchemist William
  Blomfild a Polychronicon from St Augustine's, "raising the fascinating possibility that the two men
  at some point discussed alchemical secrets" (EF 203).
- Dee owned at least six copies of the pseudo-Lullian *De secretis naturae* by 1583 (R2012a 500).
- He took about 800 books to the Continent (R2012a 504). Dee "lent him [Kelley] books, and
  translated the Opuscule of Daniel Zeccaire for him out of French" (R2012a 503).

**Dee as reader of Ripley** (detail in `RIPLEY.md` §6): annotated *Accurtations* (Wellcome 239),
*Compound* (e Musaeo 63), Bosome Book redactions (Ashmole 1486 pt. 5, under "Liber librum
apperit"), 1581 practice guided by the *Philorcium*, and the 8 Feb 1588 sericon distillation at
Třeboň recorded in diary and in a jointly signed note "J:D. E:K." (R2012a 500, 504–505; EF 296–299).
Signing was typical of Dee's annotation, "but it was rare for him to share the credit" (EF 297).

**Dee as promoter of English alchemy abroad.** Rampling's thesis: "the spread of Ripley's alchemy in
mainland Europe is intimately related to the circulation of Dee's books and Kelley's burgeoning
alchemical practice" (R2012a 499). But the promotion was mostly Kelley's: within Kelley's circle
"John Dee himself has so far played a surprisingly modest role" (R2012a 503), because of Kelley's
celebrity and his strategy of disseminating texts (R2012a 503). Dee's role was as source: Kelley's
knowledge "he owed ... to Dee" (R2012a 503), and Dee's copy of the Bosome Book was "a likely source"
for the *Viaticum* that then circulated in Bohemia (R2012a 505). Only one recipe pairs them: "Collecta
ex ore Johan: Dee & Edoardi Kyllaei Anglorum" (R2012a 503 n. 46).

**The reversal.** By 1587 "Kelley was the dominant practitioner, while Dee, in a reversal of their
earlier fortunes, increasingly performed the role of his assistant" (R2012a 506); Dee advised on
"the most suitable shape of bricks for his furnace" (EF 293). The 10 May 1588 "great secret" marks
"Dee's gradual demotion from patron to assistant" (EF 299–300).

**Dee and the English pantheon in print.** Rabbards's 1591 *Compound* praised "M. Doctor Dee in his
Monas Hyerogliphica" for "depth of learning Theoricall"; a prefatory poem by "J.D. gent:" may be
Dee's (R2012a 506 and n. 65; EF 317 n. 110 says "entirely plausible"). Ashmole printed Dee's
*Testament* (1568) from Harley 2407, "Coppied from Dr Dee's owne hand" (EF 324).

**Dee and Charnock as aspirants.** Both "aspired to become Elizabeth I's own 'philosopher'" (EF 7);
Dee's model was the "Christian Aristotle" (EF 7 n. 15, citing Clulee 1988, 189–99).

## 4. Her arguments about Kelley (EF ch. 8; R2012a)

- Kelley is usually "an appendage of Dee"; Rampling studies him as a practitioner and patronage
  client in his own right (EF 291), "an alchemical reader and exegete whose success rested, at least
  in part, on his skill at translating text-based descriptions into replicable effects" (EF 287).
- His laboratory was in the gatehouse of Rožmberk's castle at Třeboň (EF 293).
- Biography points: born Worcester, possibly trained as an apothecary; a practising medium by 27
  (EF 291). Knighted and given a seat on Rudolf's privy council; estates from Rožmberk (EF 284 and
  n. 3). Arrested April 1591, held at Křivoklát (Pürglitz); his gold "was financed by loans rather
  than transmutation" (EF 285, 301). Released autumn 1593 (EF 305). Imprisoned again at Most; perhaps
  poison after a failed escape (EF 312). Death "probably on 1 November 1597 (Prinke, 2010)"
  (R2012a 500).
- Prison writings (Leipzig UB MS 0398, from Rudolf's library) found by Rampling: a treatise of 1 March
  1592; *De chymicis oratio* (June 1592); Ramist tables for the emperor (short of paper for the gold
  and silver tables); *Responsum* to the castellan Prolhofer; *Syntagma* (20 Sept 1593), printed in
  1676 as *Via humida* (EF 302–309). Kelley's list of adepts who needed long study includes the
  obscure "Brixham", known from a manuscript Dee owned (EF 305).
- Recipes recorded by his keepers: impregnation of common mercury with *mercurius solis* overnight,
  sent to Rudolf; the "Oil of Lord Rosenberg" from litharge (EF 307–308). His most famous
  demonstration was making the mercury of gold before the emperor in fifteen minutes (EF 286).
- He forged authority: the *Work of Dunstan* (Ripley's *Accurtations* stripped of Raymond and Guido)
  and the gliding-fire token written into it; "Such methods seem characteristic of Kelley, who ...
  exhibited remarkable skill at producing exactly the results his audiences expected, whether in
  alchemical experiments or angelic conversations" (EF 312).
- Kelley "did not merely read Ripleian texts; he reconstructed Ripleian practices, wrote
  commentaries on Ripleian works, and presented Ripleian works as gifts" (EF 286).

## 5. Her key concepts

- **Sericonian alchemy**: lead calx + distilled vinegar → gum → white fume → menstruum; the dominant
  English practical tradition, 15th–early 17th c. (R2012a 499; EF 92–95; Clucas 2021 pdf 2–3).
- **Practical exegesis / alchemical reading**: decoding authorities, testing, feeding results back
  into the text (EF 7; R2014 title; Clucas 2021 pdf 2).
- **Alchemical philosopher**: a reader-practitioner credited with insight into the stone, from
  Bacon and Dee to the clothworker Thomas Peter (EF 7).
- **Tokens**: observable effects (gliding fire, mercurial tree) that let readers check practice
  against text (EF 296, 299).
- **Mixed economy**: pre-Reformation alchemy among monks, merchants and lay patrons; the Dissolution
  as "pivot point" (Clucas 2021 pdf 3, quoting EF 168, 62).
