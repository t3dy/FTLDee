## 1. The man

- A canon regular of the Augustinian priory of St Mary, Bridlington (East Yorkshire), on his own
  testimony in the *Compound*; a papal letter of 1458/9 names a canon "George Ryphey" and licenses
  him to leave Bridlington "to dwell for seven years in a university, even without the realm of
  England, and study theology" (EF 73–74).
- Claimed to have learned his alchemy in Italy: the *Compound*'s title verses say "afft er hys
  lernyng in Italye" and the *Medulla* preface speaks of nine years in Italy and nearby (EF 74).
  The *Epistle to Edward IV* instead mentions Louvain, which is one reason its attribution is
  unsure (EF 79–80).
- "Exnyng" in the *Compound* is most likely Exning in Suffolk, near Cambridge, not Oxford or Italy
  (EF 75). One Ripleian text calls him farmer and curate, possibly of Flixborough (EF 75).
- His family were Lancastrian gentry of Yorkshire and Lincolnshire, ruined after the 1469–70
  risings against Edward IV; the *Medulla* epilogue asks a bishop to receive him back into a
  cloister (EF 76–77). The bishop was assumed by the late 16th century to be George Neville,
  Archbishop of York (EF 80).
- Dates: the last contemporary record of him is the 1476 colophon of the *Medulla*; the death date
  1490 comes from sixteenth-century vitae (EF 128). The early modern legend of a royal and papal
  adviser who gave £100,000 a year to the Knights of Rhodes was developed by Ashmole and later
  writers (EF 78 and n. 55).
- Rampling's verdict on his patronage career: the *Compound* made him famous but "there is no
  evidence that it gained him any material benefits" (EF 80).

**Which are really Ripley's?** Rampling's stated position (R2021 pdf 3): the *Compound*, the
*Medulla* and at least parts of the Bosome Book are Ripley's; the *Epistle* could plausibly be;
the *Pupilla* is probably pseudepigraphic and the *Philorcium* "quite likely pseudepigraphic".
Newman (2021 pdf 2–5) argues that common authorship even of the *Compound* and the *Medulla* is not
established (see §8).

**The Ripley Scrolls.** Emblematic rolls, "deliberately archaic in appearance", copying English
verses and imagery from a late 15th-c. exemplar; the earliest is Bodleian, Bodley Rolls 1, most
copies are late 16th–17th c. (EF 253 and n. 13). The CRC excludes them: they are composite works
"seldom attributed to Ripley prior to the seventeenth century" (CRC 134). Rampling's book on the
Scrolls, *The Hidden Stone*, was forthcoming in 2020 (EF 253 n. 13) and is **not on disk**.
Found by full-text search (not by filename): Rampling's chapter "Art and Representation: The
Alchemical Image in the Islamic and Christian Middle Ages", in C. Burnett and S. Moureau (eds),
*A Cultural History of Chemistry in the Middle Ages* (Bloomsbury; year not checked on the title
page), 149–178, file `E:\pdf\Islamicate Chill Pills\Burnett\[The Cultural Histories Series] ...pdf`
(printed page = pdf − 15). On the Scroll (172–173): the earliest witness is Bodley Rolls 1, "over
twenty copies were made during the sixteenth century and later"; the roll is read top down, black
to white to red, one panel at a time; a philosopher shows a disciple the book of seven seals; the
chemical wedding becomes Adam and Eve with a half-dragon, half-woman serpent; a dying toad is
putrefaction, a dragon devours sun and moon, the Bird of Hermes eats its own wings; a red toad
stands for the base metal, and a vine with grapes replaces the apple, a clue to the vegetable stone
made from wine. Later copyists dropped the verses, "leaving just the decontextualized images"
(173). Ripley's name became attached to the Scroll only in the sixteenth century (199 n. 9).

**The Wheel (*Coelum philosophorum*).** Rampling argues the *Compound* was meant to be read with
a circular figure appended to it (R2013 45). The figure nests an inner circle within ten
concentric spheres; its "stars" are the four metallic bodies of Ripley's alchemy, equated to
gold, silver, copper and mercury (R2013 56–57). The verse attached reads "Our heaven this Figure
called is / Our table also of the lower Astronomy" (EF 310; R2013 57). The sphere of Venus marks
the Green Lion, the imperfect body that mediates between Sun and Moon (R2013 58). The
*Compound*'s "Recapitulation" sends the red man and his white wife round the wheel of seasons from
marriage in the west to ascension in the south (R2013 53); in most copies West is at the right and
South at the top (R2013 54). Before this article the Wheel's authenticity "has not been previously
established" (R2013 53 n. 26).

## 3. What Ripley's alchemy did: sericon and the two fires

- English pseudo-Lullian commentary turns on two waters or "fires" with contrary actions: the
  *ignis contra naturam*, a toxic corrosive good for gold-making, and the *ignis naturae*, which
  heals metals and the human body (R2012a 499).
- **Sericon.** In the *Medulla*'s vegetable stone, "Take the sharpest humidity of grapes" (distilled
  wine vinegar) and dissolve in it the body calcined to red, "which by masters is called sericon"
  (EF 92). Sericon nearly always means minium, red lead, in 15th-c. English texts (EF 92). The
  solution is evaporated to a gum (sugar of lead); dry distillation gives a faint water, then a
  white fume (*fumus albus*) that condenses into the "stinking menstruum", so volatile it must be
  used within the hour, and which boils on its own when poured back on its calx (EF 93). Modern
  identification: lead acetate, distilling to acetone (EF 94 and n. 97; Newman 2021 pdf 2 agrees on
  "impure acetone").
- Ripley's sericon was probably impure: dissolved, it turns green (the "Green Lion"), which fits a
  lead ore containing copper; Bridlington priory owned a lead mine (EF 94–95). In the *Compound* he
  writes of "our fine Red Lead", a hint that it is a cover name (EF 95).
- Rampling's term "sericonian" names this lead-and-vinegar method (R2012a 499). The vegetable stone
  made this way was treated as safe to drink and was one basis for *aurum potabile* "an objective
  which cannot have been assisted, in practice, by the use of toxic lead compounds" (R2012a 499).
  In the *Medulla* the vegetable stone is combined with the mineral fire against nature (cinnabar,
  vitriol, saltpetre) to make the *aqua composita* (R2012a 499).
- The reading of sericon drifted: sometimes verdigris; from the mid-16th c. often antimony
  (R2012a 499 n. 10). By 1683 the antimonial reading was printed as "Sericon or Antimony" (EF 340).
- **Mercurialism.** Late medieval transmutation theory often required "mercury alone" as the prime
  ingredient (Thorndike's term; "mercurialist" in Newman and Principe), but in practice "a
  diversity of practices in fact seems to have been the standard" in late medieval England (EF
  13–14). Rampling notes Ripley himself uses the rhetoric of "mercury alone" while his sources offer
  many menstrua (R2021 pdf 4).
- **The gliding fire.** A practica in the Whole Work: the black dregs left after distilling the
  gum (the "Black Dragon"), spread on a stone and touched with a coal, glow and calcine to a citrine
  colour within half an hour (EF 274). Rampling reproduced it with litharge: the finely divided lead
  re-oxidises (EF 274, fig. 11). Hot dregs exposed to air can catch fire without a coal (EF 281).
  Rampling calls such reproducible effects "tokens" that told reader-practitioners they were on the
  right path (EF 296, 299).

## 4. Reception in England

- The *Compound* was by the 1560s one of the best known alchemical works in England (R2012a 500).
- 1550s–60s: the corpus was "Englished"; the *Compound*, *Epistle*, *Pupilla* and *Accurtations*
  formed the basis of Ripley's reputation in the 1560s (EF 215). Ripley's name dominates the
  manuscripts of Elizabethan suitors such as Eden, Charnock and Walton (EF 206).
- Samuel Norton (1548–1621) found the Bosome Book, translated it 5 Feb 1573/4, and built his *Key of
  Alchemy* (dedicated to Elizabeth, 20 July 1577) on it (EF 253, 259–261; R2012a 504). He defends
  Ripley: "I never yet found false Conclusion in Riplie" (EF 258) and calls him almost "Raimonds
  Ape" who yet proved everything to the uttermost (EF 258). Norton also hailed him "the Raymond of
  the English" (EF 73).
- Edward Cradock translated the *Somnium* from the Bosome Book on 4 June 1582 (R2012a 505). Roger
  Howes made a second translation of the Book for Gawin Smith, finished 24 July 1593 (R2012a 505;
  EF 262).
- Ralph Rabbards printed the *Compound* in 1591, "the first time that an English vernacular
  alchemical work had been printed in its original language", dedicating it to Elizabeth and
  praising "M. Doctor Dee in his Monas Hyerogliphica" (R2012a 506; EF 317). The volume prints a
  poem by "Sr. E. K." to "G.S. Gent." (R2012a 506) and Norton's English *Vision* from the Bosome
  Book (R2012a 506 n. 67).
- Ashmole's *Theatrum Chemicum Britannicum* (1652) reinforced the English pantheon (Clucas 2021
  pdf 4); Ashmole copied Ripley's retraction from "a vellum Manuscript of Dr. Dees" (EF 324).

## 5. Reception abroad

- Most of the core corpus circulated in Latin in France and Italy by the early 1570s; the
  *Compound* as *Liber duodecim portarum* by 1571 (EF 288–289). Printed: Penot (Frankfurt 1595,
  abbreviated prose), Barnaud (*Quadriga aurifera*, Leiden 1599) (R2012a 501).
- Libavius gave the *Liber duodecim portarum* a 37-page folio commentary, allowing Ripley to be
  "the first of the best who have written of chrysopoeia . . . or to be not far from the first"
  (EF 289). Du Chesne ranked him with Lull, Bacon, Rupescissa (EF 289).
- In Maier's *Symbola aureae mensae* (1617) the English seat at the golden table goes to Roger
  Bacon, not Ripley (R2012b 480).
- Bohemia: nine Ripleian works were copied by Simon Thadeas Budek in 1605–06, with a 31-folio index
  of Ripley's terms (R2012a 501). Nicolaus Mai turned the *Liber* into elegiac verse for Rudolf II
  "at Kelley's instigation" (R2012a 501–502; EF 293–294). Mai's dedication answers those who think
  "the books of Ripley vain dreams" (EF 294).
- Ludwig Combach, physician at Hesse-Kassel, printed Kelley's letter fragments (1647) and then
  Ripley's *Opera omnia chemica* (Kassel, 1649), twelve texts, eight of them from a manuscript once
  Mai's (Kassel 4° MS chem. 67) (R2012a 503; EF 315–316). It was the first and only collected
  edition (EF 316). Combach credited Kelley with the Latin *Compound*; for the *Compound* and
  *Epistle* "this attribution is spurious" (R2012a 501).
- Hermann Conring attacked the pseudo-Lullian *De secretis naturae* in 1648 as "full of follies and
  vanities"; Combach's edition defended the Lullian line (EF 316).

## 6. Dee and Ripley

- 1556: Dee's list of alchemical authors read from July 1556 includes Ripley and Norton (R2012a 498;
  Clulee, *Ambix* 52.3 [2005] 199). The item "Ripley, anglice" is, Rampling argues, really Norton's
  *Ordinal* (R2012a 500 n. 14).
- Dee transcribed the *Accurtations* with copious annotations (Wellcome MS 239, pp. 1–44; CRC 1.9),
  cross-referencing "Riplay in his 12 Ga[tes] in the Chap. of Ferm[entation]" (R2012a 500; CRC 139).
- He owned and annotated a mid-16th-c. *Compound* (Bodleian MS e Musaeo 63) (R2012a 500; CRC 155).
- 1581, Mortlake: his laboratory notebook (Bodleian MS Rawlinson D.241, 22 June–6 Oct 1581) follows
  the *Philorcium*, not overcharging the water "as Riplay in philortium warnes of" (R2012a 500;
  EF 296).
- He copied two English redactions of the Bosome Book into Ashmole 1486 pt. 5 (the Whole Work, here
  "George Ryppleys bosome booke or Vade mecum", and the *Practise by experience*), interpreting
  sericon as antimony, under the motto "Liber librum apperit" (R2012a 504; CRC 187, 198; R2014 28;
  EF 340). He also copied "Rypleyes Vegetable water ... out of his Philorsum" (CRC 186).
- Dee's English friends handled the Bosome Book: Cradock (1582) and Gawin Smith, who visited Dee in
  Bremen in October 1589 and petitioned for him in July 1590 (R2012a 505). Rampling infers that Dee
  owned a copy and took it to Bohemia among his c. 800 books (R2012a 505); this is her inference
  from the Harley 2411 notes and the Viaticum, since Dee's copy does not survive (R2012a 505).
- 8 Feb 1588, Třeboň: Kelley "sent for me to his laboratory over the gate: to se[e] how hee |
  distilled sericon, according as in tyme past & of late he hard [sic] of me out of Riplay" (Dee's
  diary, quoted R2012a 505; EF 297). The same experiment is recorded in a marginal note signed
  "J:D. E:K." in Harley 2411 (2 lb of sericon gave 4 oz of red wine or oil) and a sketch of the
  crystalline ring: "So in a circle aboue [th]e matter was the cleare matter lyke [mercury]"
  (EF 297–299). Rampling notes the recipe calls for a golden oil, not red, so they may have been
  working from other sources (EF 299).

## 7. Kelley and Ripley

- Kelley owed his book-knowledge chiefly to Dee, who lent him books and translated Zacaire's
  *Opuscule* for him from French (R2012a 503).
- Kelley used Ripley "not only as sources of practical information, but as a means of furthering
  his own relationships" (R2012a 498): he had Mai versify the *Compound* and wrote a punning
  distich for it (R2012a 502; EF 294); on 2 Aug 1589 he gave Karl von Biberstein, master of the
  Bohemian mint, a compendium of seven Ripleian works and adopted him as "philosophical son"
  (R2012a 502; EF 295).
- The *Work of Dunstan* is a Latin translation of the *Accurtations* with Raymond and Guido removed
  to make it look older; the *Clavis aureae portae* is the *Work of Dunstan* re-attributed to
  Ripley (EF 290; R2012a 503 n. 38). Both first appear in east-central Europe in the late 1580s,
  when Kelley was at Třeboň (EF 290).
- Prison writings (Leipzig UB MS 0398, from Rudolf II's library): Kelley's treatises from Křivoklát
  (Pürglitz), 1592–93, rest on Ripley and Dunstan; his *Syntagma* (20 Sept 1593) became the printed
  *Via humida* (1676) (EF 302–309). He paraphrases Ripley's lower astronomy through Saturn/lead as
  the outermost metal whose vinegary menstruum dissolves the rest in order (EF 309–311), and writes
  the gliding fire into his recipes and into the *Work of Dunstan* (EF 311–312).

## 8. Disagreements and contradictions (recorded, not resolved)

1. **Who wrote the corpus.** Newman: the *Compound* never uses "sericon", uses Green Lion for
   copper, and its "Admonition" demands a "naturall Mercuryalyte" from the human body; common
   authorship with the *Medulla* is unproven without critical editions (Newman 2021 pdf 2–5).
   Rampling: in the *Compound* "Man" and "blood" are cover names, and Ripley always rejects literal
   blood; Green Lion denotes the imperfect metal in both works, so there is "no inconsistency that
   would be fatal to shared authorship" (R2021 pdf 5–6).
2. **Ripley's dates.** Clucas gives "George Ripley (1415–1490)" (Clucas 2021 pdf 1). Rampling: "fl.
   1470s", last record 1476, 1490 only from later vitae (EF 2, 128); "d. c. 1490" (R2012b 477).
   No source on disk documents a birth year of 1415.
3. **Dee's annotation of MS e Musaeo 63.** R2012a 500: annotated "on 21 December 1595". CRC 155
   (entry 9.26): "Annotation by ... John Dee (dated 1597)". Same manuscript, two dates.
4. **What was distilled on 8 Feb 1588.** Dee's diary says sericon (R2012a 505; EF 297; Fenton,
   *Diaries* 233). Parry writes that Kelley "distilled antimony, following George Ripley's 'Bosom
   Book', which Dee read aloud" (Parry, *Arch-Conjuror* 201). Rampling shows the antimonial reading
   of sericon is a later reinterpretation that Dee himself wrote into his copy (R2014 28; EF 340),
   and that the 15th-c. sense was red lead (EF 92). **[inference]** Parry has silently applied
   Dee's gloss; "read aloud" paraphrases "heard of me out of Riplay".
5. **Kelley and the *Work of Dunstan*.** In 2012 Rampling says it is only "conceivable" that Kelley
   helped compose the *Work* or the *Clavis* (R2012a 503 n. 38); in 2020 she argues his "probable
   role in adapting the Work of Dunstan" from the gliding-fire passage (EF 312). Fenton's note calls
   the Dunstan book "almost certainly written by Kelly himself" (Fenton, *Diaries* 61 n. 9), while
   Whitby reports Fell Smith's suggestion that it was a copy of a treatise "formerly believed to be
   the work of that saint" (Whitby, *John Dee's Actions with Spirits* 59–60).
6. **The Philorcium.** Dee treated it as Ripley's practical authority in 1581 (R2012a 500); Rampling
   now judges it "quite likely pseudepigraphic" (R2021 pdf 3). Not a disagreement between scholars
   but a gap between Dee's belief and modern attribution.
