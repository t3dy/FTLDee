## 0. The finding that matters most

The documented record mostly shows **Dee being watched, not watching**. The people around him who
were really intelligencers are named in the sources: Charles Sled, William Herle, Thomas Watson,
Francis Garland, Edward Whitlock alias "John Basset", Arthur de Champernon and Stephen Powle. Dee was
the **subject** of their reports. Szőnyi puts it as "it was not he who was spying, but rather he who
became the prey of the spies of Rome" (Szőnyi 258–259). Parry, on Dee's 1589 reports about the
Jesuit Christopher Parkins, writes: "This must finally silence the old canard that Dee somehow spied
for Walsingham" (Parry 204).

What Dee did that resembles intelligence work is real, but it is the work of an **expert consultant
and correspondent**, an "intelligencer" in Sherman's broad sense of "a retailer of special (often
secret) knowledge" (Sherman 1995, xiii). Sherman adds that such brokers were "both necessary and
necessarily invisible" (Sherman 1995, 25–26).

## 2. What the record does **not** contain

- **No commission, payment or instruction from Walsingham for espionage or cipher work.** The
  documented commissions are medical (1578), legal and imperial (1577–78), calendrical (1583),
  navigational (1583) and alchemical (1590). The Dee–Walsingham correspondence from abroad is
  self-promotion and denunciation (Parry 192, 204).
- **No cipher made by Dee for state use.** The only Dee codes documented are the diary scripts (#26)
  and the simple codes inside the angelic actions, which Dee solved quickly (Whitby 105).
- **No Action in Burghley's or Walsingham's papers.** Whitby: the likely recipients of coded actions
  would be Burghley and Walsingham, and "there are no records of Dee's Actions" in their papers
  (Whitby 105).
- **No "007".** See `SPY_LEGEND.md`.

## 3. Disagreement among scholars

| Question | Position A | Position B |
|---|---|---|
| Was Dee a Walsingham agent abroad? | **No.** Parry 204 ("old canard"); Szőnyi 258–259 (Dee the prey, not the spy); Whitby 104–105 | **Partly.** Baldwin (Clucas ed. 2006, 106): Deacon's hypothesis is "only partially substantiated", by Dee's knowledge of ciphers and one letter to Walsingham from Třeboň. But Baldwin, at 118, also finds it "difficult to suggest" Dee was better briefed "as a secret agent of Walsingham's" than his learning explains. Marshall (popular history): "There may have been good grounds for suspecting" it (ch. 9) |
| Did Dee understand the *Steganographia* as cryptography? | **No**, he read it as angel magic (Parry 50) | **Yes, in part**: "his evident knowledge of ciphers" (Baldwin 106); Hooke and the legend assume so |
| Do Dee's letters from abroad hide secret business? | **Self-promotion and fear** (Parry 192) | "Veiled allusions to secret affairs" are the ground of the spy supposition (Fell Smith 89, reporting the theory, not endorsing it) |
