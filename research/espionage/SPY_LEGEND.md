## 2. Genealogy of the legend

1. **Contemporaries, 1584–86: an accusation, not a legend.** At the Spanish ambassador's table in
   Prague, a Dutchman calls Dee a "spy sent by Walsingham" (Marshall ch. 9, PDF p. 129). The nuncios'
   party spreads the rumour that he is the Queen's secret agent (Marshall ch. 9, PDF p. 133). Dee
   himself says he was "pried and peered into" by "honourable espies" (Fenton 207–209).
2. **Robert Hooke, 1690**, "Of Dr Dee's Book of Spirits" (*Posthumous Works*, 1705, 203–10). Hooke
   holds that the spirit actions "are all Cryptography", and so is part of the travel journal (Whitby
   104). He says Dee posed as a "Pretended Enthusiast" because prosecution would be gentler than for
   "a real Spy" (Brann 235, quoting Hooke). He also suggests the Holy Table hid apparatus "to make
   Apparitions" (Henry, Hooke chapter, PDF p. 274). Fenton: "the mistaken theory that Dee was in
   reality a government spy using an elaborate code" (Fenton 128–130).
3. **Adam Clarke** (c. 1762–1832) took up Hooke's theory and "failed to prove any substance" (Whitby
   104). Isaac D'Israeli discussed it in 1841 (Whitby 113 n.10).
4. **Fell Smith, 1909**, reports "the supposition" that Dee was Walsingham's "secret spy and
   diplomatic agent abroad" and that "his cabalistic diagrams contained a cipher", resting on his
   letters' "veiled allusions to secret affairs". She calls it "an elaborate theory" and does not
   adopt it (Fell Smith 89). **So the idea predates Deacon.**
5. **Richard Deacon (Donald McCormick), 1968.** He revives Hooke: "for Deacon, Dee was an Elizabethan
   007 engaged in espionage for England as an early modern James Bond" (Clulee 2015, 229–230). He
   prints a supposedly coded action from "Divers Curious Narrations of Doctor John Dee", said to be a
   Russian MS by Arthur Dee translated by "Prince Belloselski". Whitby could not trace it, and
   Deacon's publishers did not answer him (Whitby 113–114).
6. **After 1968.** Woolley (2001) suggests "clandestine involvements". Clulee: "Deacon's ghost also
   haunts" them (Clulee 2015, 230). Marshall (2006) allows "good grounds for suspecting" (ch. 9, PDF
   p. 134, citing Woolley 271). The web repeats Deacon without sources (§4).

Other verdicts on Deacon:

- Heilbron: Dee "was not the CIA man that Richard Deacon portrays in his worthless fiction… Deacon
  has all Dee's gullibility, but none of his learning" (Heilbron, introductory essay to Shumaker ed.,
  *John Dee on Astronomy*, 1978, p. 35 n.107).
- Clulee: in Deacon, "Dee as 'secret agent' dominates the interpretive framework", with speculation
  playing as large a role as in a novel (Clulee 1988, 242 n.3).
- Clulee again: "the sensationalism of Deacon's secret agent" (Clulee in Clucas ed. 2006, 34).
- Hutton wishes Deacon's quotation of Dee "provided a reference to the original source" (Hutton,
  *Witches, Druids and King Arthur*, 304 n.19).
- Peter French, as quoted in the swantower.com review: "riddled with factual inaccuracies".
  **Not verified first-hand.**

**Contradiction worth recording.** Håkansson still quotes Deacon for a description of Pickering
(Håkansson 8 n.9). Deacon leaks into scholarly footnotes for small details.
