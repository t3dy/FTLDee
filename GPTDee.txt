The main opportunity I see is to make the assets less like “background art for a historical game” and more like the actual material infrastructure of Dee’s intellectual career. Your existing design already has the right conceptual foundation: books are capabilities, people are alternative ways of performing operations, and stations train skills. CLAUDE The library research suggests that you can push that considerably further.

One particularly strong finding is that Dee's material world was unusually legible through things: manuscripts with annotations, catalogues with acquisition marks, instruments, diagrams, correspondence, booksellers, patrons, laboratories, and copying activity. Sophie Page's discussion of Dee's Canterbury/St. Augustine's manuscripts is especially useful here: Dee did not simply possess “magic books”; he tracked a dispersed collection, marked what he had acquired, bracketed things he wanted, underlined particular works, and annotated some manuscripts extensively.  That gives you several new asset mechanics.

## 1. Locations: make them resource ecologies, not scenery

Your 12-location structure is sound, but I would give each location a second layer of “what can actually be obtained or accomplished here.”

For Mortlake, for example, don't just have:

> Library → Manuscript Knowledge +1

Instead, the Library could contain:

- Dee's working catalogue
- acquired manuscripts
- books awaiting copying
- books awaiting annotation
- correspondence awaiting replies
- desiderata — books Dee wants but does not own
- astronomical tables
- loose mathematical diagrams
- patron letters
- household accounts
- controversial texts that cannot safely be displayed to visitors

This fits the evidence that Dee's library was not simply a static collection. Page describes his ownership of the St. Augustine's catalogue itself and his marking of volumes he had acquired or hoped to acquire. Sophie Page Magic in the Cloist…

That gives you a really nice FTL-style mechanic:

**Library state = Repertoire + Desiderata + Unfinished Intellectual Work.**

A library visit could produce something like:

> **ST. AUGUSTINE'S CATALOGUE**
>
> You discover three volumes from the old Canterbury collection that you have not yet acquired.
>
> [Purchase] [Send a contact] [Copy relevant passages] [Mark for later]

That is much more interesting than “buy +1 occult book.”

The same principle applies elsewhere:

**London** → books, printers, booksellers, financial contacts, rumors, instruments, paper, binding materials.

**Greenwich** → access, patronage, court intelligence, demonstrations, royal attention.

**Barn Elms** → information, ciphering, intelligence, correspondence, dangerous rumors.

**Prague** → specialists, court patronage, alchemical apparatus, manuscripts, rival intellectual networks.

**Mortlake** → synthesis: the place where everything you've acquired becomes usable.

This reinforces your existing distinction between intellectual capability and political access. CLAUDE

## 2. Buildings/stations: give every station an actual “work product”

This is probably the biggest mechanical refinement.

At present the station is essentially:

> crew → skill bonus

I'd make it:

> crew + station + asset → work product

For example:

### Mortlake Scriptorium

Assets:

- exemplar manuscript
- paper
- parchment
- quills
- ink
- copying desk
- binding tools
- scribal model
- catalogue

Possible outputs:

- copied manuscript
- corrected manuscript
- fair copy of Dee's treatise
- extract from a difficult source
- duplicate for a patron
- annotated working copy

And importantly, the person assigned there changes the product.

Roger Cooke might make copying more efficient; another specialist might improve Latin transcription; Dee himself might produce annotations rather than copies.

This makes “crew placement” much more interesting because the player isn't merely deciding where to put the +1 character. The question becomes:

> What do I need produced this month?

That is exactly compatible with your existing station architecture, where crew presence trains skills and unlocks location-specific encounters. WORLDBUILDING

### Laboratory

Likewise, don't make it simply “Alchemy +1.”

Give it apparatus slots:

- furnace
- athanor
- alembic
- retort
- crucible
- vessels
- scales
- materials
- written recipe/procedure

Then an alchemical operation requires an apparatus configuration.

The visual asset becomes mechanically meaningful.

## 3. Books: this is where I would radically expand the asset model

The current book schema is good but too bibliographical. WORLDASSETS

I'd distinguish:

**Book as physical object**

from

**Book as intellectual repertoire**

and

**Book as working copy.**

A book could have:

```text
BOOK
 ├── bibliographic identity
 ├── edition
 ├── physical form
 ├── provenance
 ├── ownership history
 ├── condition
 ├── annotations
 ├── extracted operations
 ├── associated people
 └── acquisition status
```

This is strongly supported by the Page material.

Dee's Canterbury manuscripts weren't interchangeable “occult books.” Some contained natural magic, image magic, ritual magic, lapidaries, etc.; Dee annotated some of them, and in alchemical sections he drew diagrams of operations, used planetary shorthand, and decoded words. 

That gives you a fantastic game asset:

### Annotated Manuscript

A manuscript might begin:

> **Liber de XV Stellis**

and after Dee studies it:

> **Liber de XV Stellis — Dee's Working Copy**

with newly unlocked:

- stellar correspondences
- stones
- herbs
- images
- planetary operations

The book literally changes as the player works on it.

You could even have three states:

**Unexamined → Annotated → Operationalized**

That would make your “books are professional capabilities” principle extremely concrete. CLAUDE

## 4. Add “Book Fragments” as an asset class

This follows directly from Dee's manuscript culture.

Not every acquisition should be a whole book.

Possible assets:

- copied folio
- excerpt
- diagram
- recipe
- table
- cipher
- marginal note
- translated passage
- scholium
- lost-book reference
- catalogue entry
- bibliographical lead

This could become one of the most distinctive mechanics in the game.

For instance:

> **You cannot acquire Liber Razielis.**
>
> But a scholar has offered to let you copy six folios.

That isn't equivalent to owning the book.

You have:

**Knowledge fragment: Razielis / angelic names / ritual structure**

rather than:

**Book: Liber Razielis**

That distinction would be historically appropriate and mechanically useful.

Page specifically notes Dee's interest in the *Ars notoria* and *Liber Razielis*, including his use of catalogue markings to track works he wanted. Sophie Page Magic in the Cloist…

## 5. Printing presses: turn printing into an operation

The current press category is promising, but I wouldn't treat a printer as merely a place where a book is produced.

Make printing a chain:

**manuscript → fair copy → printer → type → compositor → proof → correction → paper → press → binding → patron/distribution**

This gives you an entire production mini-system.

Assets could include:

- type cases
- individual type sorts
- printer's ornaments
- woodcuts
- title-page frames
- mathematical diagrams
- compositor's desk
- ink
- paper
- proof sheets
- correction marks
- binding tools
- printer's device

And different printers have different capabilities.

A mathematical book might require a printer capable of setting complicated diagrams. An occult work might require discretion. A royal work might require patronage.

This fits your publishing theme, which the biography system already identifies as a distinct aspect of Dee's career. BIOGRAPHY

## 6. Scientific instruments: don't make them equipment bonuses

This is another place where the game could distinguish itself.

An instrument should be an **operation interface**.

Instead of:

> Astrolabe +2 Astronomy

make it:

> Astrolabe → permits specific observational operations.

Possible instrument assets:

- astrolabe
- armillary sphere
- celestial globe
- terrestrial globe
- quadrant
- cross-staff
- compass
- proportional/dividing instruments
- surveying instruments
- perspective devices
- optical instruments
- astronomical tables
- clocks/timekeeping devices

Then give each:

```text
instrument
→ required knowledge
→ possible operations
→ required location
→ possible outputs
```

Your existing research image actually gives you an excellent visual vocabulary here: instruments, globes, compasses, geometric solids, charts and papers are all grouped as parts of Dee's mathematical arts rather than isolated “science equipment.” 

So an instrument workshop could let you:

**Observe → Calculate → Demonstrate → Teach → Navigate → Construct → Impress a patron**

That is much more Dee-like than RPG equipment.

## 10. Libraries: make them different from inventories

I would make libraries one of the most important systems in the game.

A library should have:

**Collection**

What exists here.

**Catalogue**

What you know exists.

**Access**

What you are allowed to inspect.

**Ownership**

What you actually possess.

**Desiderata**

What you want.

**Annotations**

What you have learned.

**Copying**

What you have reproduced.

**Provenance**

Where things came from.

**Reputation**

What scholars associate with the collection.

This is unusually well supported by the Dee material. Page's account of the St. Augustine's collection gives you almost the whole system: Dee had the catalogue, identified what he possessed, marked things he hoped to acquire, and paid particular attention to selected works. Sophie Page Magic in the Cloist…

This could become a miniature strategy game inside FTLDee.

## 17. One important historical cleanup

There are a few things in the current WORLDASSETS draft that I'd correct before Claude starts generating assets.

The most obvious is **Greenwich Observatory**. The catalog itself calls it anachronistic: Flamsteed's Greenwich Observatory dates to 1676, after Dee's period. WORLDASSETS I'd remove it from the Dee-era asset set rather than deliberately creating an anachronistic building.

Likewise, the current Turka-world **Isfahan/Safavid palace** needs revision. Safavid Isfahan belongs to a much later period than Ibn Turka. Since the whole system is explicitly committed to “real places, real history” with historical-status tagging, this is exactly the sort of thing the asset audit should catch. WORLDBUILDING

More generally, I'd change the asset rule to:

> **Historical object first; visual approximation second.**

If we don't know what a specific Dee-era building looked like, create an evidence-backed *type* rather than inventing a falsely precise reconstruction.

The documents fall into three layers: the original game concept/architecture, the current v0.2 rules, and the historical/audit documents that are now being used to refine the rules. Here is what each contributes.

### `DESIGN(9).md` — the original game design

This is the conceptual foundation. It defines FTLDee as a procedural historical career RPG combining FTL's node-map/crew/pressure structure with relationship management, deckbuilding and historical bibliography. The central premise is that Dee is trying to become useful to courts and patrons rather than defeat enemies. DESIGN(9)

Its most important design idea is:

> **BOOK + SKILL + CONTACT + PATRON = OPERATION**

Books are therefore not generic equipment; they expose professional/intellectual operations, which require the appropriate competence and social opportunity to deploy.

It also establishes the intellectual labor-market concept of the map, multidimensional reputation, Mortlake as the FTL “ship,” and crew as alternative ways of performing operations rather than stat bonuses. DESIGN(9)

So this is essentially the game's **design philosophy**.

### `WORLDBUILDING.md` — the shared world architecture

This expands the original concept into the geography and ontology underlying both the Dee and Ibn Turka games.

The major innovation is that locations are hierarchical rather than simply map nodes. A location contains stations, residents, encounters, access requirements and travel properties. Crew occupy physical stations and thereby train skills or unlock encounters. WORLDBUILDING

It also establishes the Dee ↔ Turka shared-world concept: historically separate periods can be connected through explicitly marked counterfactual routes, while locations, stations and crew mechanics remain reusable.

The important principle here is:

> **Crew is spatial, not abstract.**

This is the document that makes Mortlake, Samarkand Observatory, Prague, etc. into actual playable environments rather than destinations on a travel graph.

### `WORLDASSETS.md` — the asset-production ontology

This is the practical inventory of what has to be created to render that world: locations, buildings, books, instruments, printing presses, maps, reference imagery, audio and associated provenance.

The crucial contribution is provenance tracking and historical-status tagging. Assets are not simply “make an image of Mortlake”; they need to distinguish documented, plausible and counterfactual material.

This is therefore the **art/content production bible** rather than a mechanics document.

The earlier handoff explicitly describes it as the inventory of world asset types, including locations, buildings and printing presses. HANDOVER(20261003-074632)

### `HANDOVER(20261003-074632).md` — the project-state snapshot

This is not really a design document. It is a **session handoff**.

It records what was built during the preceding work session:

- the 58-entry Dee biography database;
- its 17 historiographical themes;
- the query API;
- the shared-world architecture;
- the location/station/crew model;
- the intended 12-location geography;
- the major implementation priorities.

The biography layer is especially important: entries are typed as people, events, documents, places, institutions and ventures, with historical-status labels and thematic tags. HANDOVER(20261003-074632)

The handoff's purpose is therefore continuity: it tells a future coding session what exists, what decisions were made, and what remains to be implemented. It should probably be treated as **project history**, not as an authoritative design specification.

### `SYSTEMS_V2.md` — the current authoritative rules

This is the most important technical document now.

It explicitly supersedes the older room/book sections of `MECHANICS_FROM_BIOGRAPHY.md` and defines the v0.2 FTL layer. Everything is a card: houses, rooms, books, instruments, crew, locations, errands, encounters, factions, skills and weather. SYSTEMS_V2

Its FTL translation is explicit:

- ship → household
- systems → rooms
- crew → household/retinue
- weapons → books
- cargo → library
- augments → instruments
- scrap → money
- hull → Secrecy
- fuel → Days
- rebel fleet → political pressure
- sector → England/Prague
- blue options → historically/intellectually gated actions. SYSTEMS_V2

It then specifies the actual rules for rooms, house tiers, Fortune, crew posting, errands, books/satchel, effective skills, time, political weather, network drift, the Ottoman flag, locations and the historical-content/speech rules.

This is the document Claude Code should treat as the **current rules authority**.

### `ARC_REPORT.md` — historical biography versus actual gameplay

This is an audit of whether the current mechanics actually reproduce Dee's career.

It walks through the career chronologically: formation, suspicion, Elizabethan election, the failed institutional ambitions, the angelic turn, emigration, Prague, and the return. For every phase it asks: what happened historically, where is it represented mechanically, and how faithfully? ARC_REPORT

The interesting result is that many things are already structurally represented but some of their historical meaning has been flattened.

For example, the angelic turn is mechanically represented by the progression:

**show-stone → Sigillum → Holy Table → Scrying Chamber**

but the game does not yet communicate *why* a mathematician and natural philosopher turns so intensely toward angelic communication. Likewise, emigration is represented by the satchel, but the household's actual movement is wrong.

This is therefore the game's **historical narrative audit**.

### `BOOKS_AS_STORY.md` — books as historical objects

This is a very focused document arguing that the game's current treatment of books is too much like FTL weapons.

The existing system models buying, carrying and selling. The document identifies the historically important verbs that are missing:

**buy, annotate, lend, catalogue, pledge, leave, plunder, burn, sell.**

It uses Dee's actual library history to argue that books need to behave as things with provenance and biography, not interchangeable cards. BOOKS_AS_STORY

It proposes, among other things:

- Soyga should not randomly appear in the London market;
- books should be purchasable even if the player cannot yet read/use them;
- books should show “Unread — needs X”;
- books could be bought on credit;
- Dee's own books should grow as scrying records accumulate;
- showing the angelic records should itself be a consequential operation;
- the library should have a history when Dee emigrates. BOOKS_AS_STORY

This is probably the document with the biggest implications for your eventual **asset ontology**.

### `CREW_AS_CHARACTERS.md` — turning historical people into game characters

This addresses the difficult problem of representing real historical people without inventing dialogue or reducing them to RPG stats.

Its fundamental rule is that a character should be recognizable from **where the player wants to post them and what happens when they are posted there**, rather than from invented personality dialogue. CREW_AS_CHARACTERS

It establishes five techniques:

- reported speech;
- properly attributed historical quotations;
- recorded angelic speech;
- anonymous characters for invented dialogue;
- letting mechanics provide characterization.

The Jane Dee section illustrates the method particularly well. Her importance comes from household stability, court knowledge, her relationship with the scrying enterprise, the historical episode of 1582, and later Prague decisions—not from giving her invented quips. CREW_AS_CHARACTERS

This is the **characterization bible**.

### `PRAGUE_AND_OTTOMAN.md` — the second sector and counterfactual branch

This is the narrative/mechanical treatment of Prague.

Its central contrast is excellent:

> **England = accumulation. Prague = dependence.**

In England Dee builds a household, library and network. In Prague he is a guest, lacks his library, depends on intermediaries and has a family to support. PRAGUE_AND_OTTOMAN

The document then maps the Prague sequence:

**hope → imperial wall → exposure → escalating angelic demands → expulsion.**

It audits the existing Prague nodes and proposes specific fixes: put the family in Prague from the beginning, represent Kraków as an expedition rather than another map sector, give the angelic prophecies a dated track, move the Mortlake-library vision to the voyage, make Kelley increasingly important to court access, and add Pucci.

It also treats the Ottoman/Constantinople thread as an explicitly counterfactual branch rather than pretending it is documented history.

This is essentially the **Prague narrative design document**.

### `ROOMS_AND_HOUSE_AS_STORY.md` — making mechanics communicate biography

This takes the eight-room household and asks a very specific question:

> Can the player infer what Mortlake meant to Dee from how the house behaves?

It argues that the basic answer is already yes: Mortlake behaves like a workshop/household that the player continually invests in, rather than like an abstract headquarters. ROOMS_AND_HOUSE_AS_STORY

Then it audits each room:

- Library already works well.
- Study is too generic.
- Scriptorium is somewhat anachronistically named.
- Correspondence works.
- Laboratory needs its continental-patron effect to become mechanically real.
- Scrying Chamber is the strongest room because its apparatus progression actually tells the story.
- Instrument Room needs actual encounters.
- Quarters can tell the household story through capacity and overcrowding. ROOMS_AND_HOUSE_AS_STORY

It also identifies three currently missing dimensions of Mortlake: it was built on credit, people visited it, and it was dismantled/looted during Dee's absence.

This is the **mechanics-as-narrative audit**.

### `PROPOSED_MECHANICS_TWEAKS.md` — the implementation backlog

This is the most concrete of the narrative documents. It takes findings from the audits and converts them into proposed mechanics.

It explicitly says these are **proposals, not decisions**, and that adopted changes should be entered into `DECISIONS.md`. PROPOSED_MECHANICS_TWEAKS

The first major proposals are:

1. Have Jane, the children and Kelleys actually cross the Channel.
2. Make the Mortlake library departure a sequence involving the catalogue, Fromond's loan and a keeper.
3. Make petitions generate promises rather than immediate cash.
4. Give the angelic/scrying system historically meaningful consequences.
5. Add the dated angelic prophecy track.
6. Represent Kraków as an expedition.
7. etc.

The distinctive feature is that each proposal specifies:

**what the player does → what it interacts with → how it is taught → historical source → legibility.**

So this is effectively the **candidate patch list for the current build**.

### `ACCURACY_FLAGS.md` — the factual error register

This is the quality-control document.

It systematically compares the current `src/data` against the Dee corpus and classifies errors as HIGH, MEDIUM or LOW. It also separates factual corrections from general inference.

The clearest example is the HIGH-severity error that the game says Dee leaves Jane and the children behind when departing England. The documentary record shows that Jane and the three children embarked with Dee; Jane was left in Prague later. ACCURACY_FLAGS

|---|---|
| **Map** | Geographic/spatial representation and node connections |
| **Buildings** | Major physical structures |
| **Stations** | Places where crew actually perform work |
| **People** | Permanent, seasonal and transient NPCs |

Yes. I would make this a dedicated **Narrative Systems / Mechanics Bible** section rather than scattering these explanations through the existing systems documents.

The important concept is that the narrative designer isn't merely documenting mechanics. They are explaining **how historical knowledge, character capabilities, objects, locations, networks, and player choices become narrative possibilities**.

A useful organizing section would look something like:

# Narrative Systems: Anatomy of Play

### 1. Anatomy of an Encounter Synthesis
Explain how an encounter is assembled from:

`LOCATION + SITUATION + PEOPLE + KNOWLEDGE + BOOKS + SKILLS + EQUIPMENT + NETWORKS + CURRENT STATE`

Then explain that the resulting choices are not arbitrary branching dialogue. Each choice is an **expression of what the player currently has access to**.

For example:

```text
ENCOUNTER
"The court asks Dee to interpret an unusual celestial phenomenon."

              │
      ┌───────┼────────┐
      ↓       ↓        ↓
  ASTRONOMY  COURT    OCCULT
    SKILL    CONTACT  KNOWLEDGE
      │       │        │
      └───────┼────────┘
              ↓
        AVAILABLE OPTIONS
```

The encounter writer should document why each option exists and what makes it available.

### 2. Choice Color: What the Player Is Actually Seeing

Define the visual language of choices.

Rather than color simply meaning "good/bad," color should communicate **the source or basis of an option**.

For example:

- **Skill-colored** — available because a crew member possesses relevant trained capability.
- **Book-colored** — available because the household owns or has consulted a particular text.
- **Instrument-colored** — requires an appropriate physical instrument.
- **Contact-colored** — available because the player knows someone who can intervene.
- **Location-colored** — enabled by being somewhere appropriate.
- **Reputation/network-colored** — available because the player's social position has changed.
- **Historical/knowledge-colored** — unlocked by information previously learned.
- **Risk/occult-colored** — represents an option associated with dangerous or uncertain knowledge.
- **Unavailable choices** — visible when useful, but clearly explain what is missing.

The bible should establish the exact palette and terminology later, but the conceptual rule is important:

> **Choice color tells the player why this choice is possible.**

That makes the UI itself part of the game's epistemology.

### 3. The Choice Is a Synthesis, Not a Button

This should be a major principle.

A choice may require several things simultaneously:

```text
BOOK
  +
SKILL
  +
INSTRUMENT
  +
PERSON
  +
LOCATION
  =
SPECIALIZED APPROACH
```

For example, a mathematical observation might require:

```text
Astronomy skill
+ astronomical tables
+ appropriate instrument
+ competent crew member
+ observation location
```

Another character might approach exactly the same encounter through:

```text
Natural Philosophy
+ relevant book
+ court contact
```

The encounter therefore becomes a **test of the player's intellectual infrastructure**, not a simple dialogue test.

### 4. Anatomy of a Choice

Every authored choice should have a small internal specification:

```text
CHOICE

Narrative proposition:
What is the character attempting?

Prerequisites:
What makes this option available?

Visual provenance:
Why is the option colored this way?

Required assets:
Books / instruments / people / contacts / location.

Relevant skills:
Which skill tree nodes contribute?

Immediate consequence:
What happens now?

Knowledge consequence:
What does the player learn?

Network consequence:
Who notices?

Material consequence:
Money / books / instruments / supplies.

Reputation consequence:
Who thinks differently of Dee?

State consequence:
What changes in the world?

Future hooks:
Which later encounters does this unlock or close?
```

That gives the narrative designer a common vocabulary with the systems designer.

### 5. Skills Are Ways of Seeing

This section should explain that the skill tree isn't primarily a conventional RPG power ladder.

A skill represents **a way of perceiving and acting upon the world**.

A character with strong mathematical ability doesn't merely receive "+10% astronomy."

They see different possibilities in an encounter.

Likewise:

```text
GEOGRAPHY
→ recognizes a navigational problem

MATHEMATICS
→ recognizes a calculational problem

NATURAL PHILOSOPHY
→ recognizes a causal/natural-philosophical problem

ALCHEMY
→ recognizes a material/process problem

LINGUISTICS
→ recognizes a textual/philological problem

COURT KNOWLEDGE
→ recognizes a social/political problem
```

This is particularly important for the Dee game because the historical subject is **intellectual labor**.

### 6. Books Are Executable Knowledge

The narrative bible should then explain the relationship between books and encounters.

A book isn't merely:

> +2 Occult

It contains **methods, authorities, terminology, diagrams, arguments and procedures** that can become encounter options.

Thus:

```text
BOOK
↓
knowledge repertoire
↓
recognition of situation
↓
new option
↓
new operation
↓
new consequence
```

This connects directly to the existing design principle:

`BOOK + SKILL + CONTACT + PATRON = OPERATION`

### 7. Instruments Are Physical Arguments

The same treatment should be given to instruments.

An astrolabe, globe, mathematical instrument, crystal, furnace, balance or other object isn't simply equipment with stats.

Possessing it means:

> Dee's household can perform something that it previously could not perform.

An instrument therefore changes the **action space** of the narrative.

### 8. Crew as Interpretive Agents

The crew section should explain why historical characters matter mechanically.

Different people should expose different possibilities because they possess:

- different skills
- different books
- different professional experience
- different relationships
- different tolerances for risk
- different historical identities
- different consequences when placed in particular situations

This is how Jane, Kelley, Cooke and other historical figures become more than portraits attached to statistics.

### 9. Locations as Knowledge Machines

A location similarly changes the available narrative vocabulary.

London provides:

```text
books
printers
merchants
information
contacts
money
manuscripts
```

Greenwich provides:

```text
court
patronage
royal access
mathematical presentation
political networks
```

Mortlake provides:

```text
library
laboratory
household
correspondence
research
scrying
```

Thus the location is not merely where an encounter happens.

**The location determines what kinds of things can happen there.**

### 10. Networks and Contacts

A contact should create **social mechanics**, not simply reputation points.

A contact can:

- introduce Dee to someone
- authenticate him
- provide information
- obtain access
- recommend him
- lend a book
- transmit a letter
- mediate a dispute
- create an obligation
- expose him to risk

Contacts therefore function similarly to books and instruments: they are **capabilities externalized into the social world**.

### 11. Consequence Chains

Narrative designers should document that an encounter has multiple consequence channels:

```text
                    ENCOUNTER
                       │
       ┌───────────────┼────────────────┐
       ↓               ↓                ↓
    MATERIAL        KNOWLEDGE         SOCIAL
       │               │                │
   money/books      skills/info       contacts
       │               │                │
       └───────────────┼────────────────┘
                       ↓
                    WORLD STATE
                       │
                       ↓
                 FUTURE ENCOUNTERS
```

This prevents the common RPG problem where every encounter ends with "+10 gold."

### 12. Historical Gating

This should explain the distinction between:

**Hard historical gates**

Things the player cannot simply invent because the historical situation makes them impossible.

**Soft historical gates**

Things that are plausible but require particular knowledge, contacts, timing or circumstances.

**Counterfactual gates**

Explicit departures from the documented historical record.

This is especially important for Prague, Constantinople and the shared Dee/Turka world.

### 13. Encounter Synthesis Example

Finally, give the narrative designer a complete worked example.

Something like:

**The 1577 Comet at Windsor**

```text
LOCATION
Windsor

SITUATION
Court wants an interpretation of an unusual celestial phenomenon.

AVAILABLE ACTORS
Dee
court intermediary
astronomical specialist

RELEVANT KNOWLEDGE
astronomy
mathematics
Aristotelian natural philosophy
astrological traditions

RELEVANT OBJECTS
astronomical tables
globe
mathematical instruments

RELEVANT NETWORK
royal access
court recommendation
```

The encounter then produces different choices depending upon the player's accumulated intellectual infrastructure.

The crucial point would be that the writer does **not** start by inventing six dialogue choices.

They start with:

> What is happening historically?  
> What could someone like Dee plausibly know?  
> What could he physically do?  
> Who could help him?  
> What objects could he use?  
> What would different intellectual approaches reveal?  
> What would the court care about?  
> What consequences would follow?

Only then do they write the choices.

That could become the core doctrine of the narrative system:

> **The game does not author choices independently of the simulation. The simulation synthesizes the choices available to the player from the intellectual, material, social and historical resources they have accumulated.**

That is the section I would have the narrative designer write into the game bible. It would give the content team a repeatable method for producing encounters rather than merely a collection of encounter examples.

Yes. That changes the narrative architecture in an important way.

The vertical slice should be understood as a **playable reconstruction of Dee's biography**, not a sandbox capable of generating arbitrary Elizabethan occult adventures.

The core relationship is:

**historical episode → historically available situation → documented decision space → player choice → historical consequence**

The player is effectively inhabiting Dee's decision-making position, but the game should not manufacture decisions that Dee never faced merely because they would make interesting gameplay.

For the narrative-design section, I would make this a foundational principle:

> **Biography is the content boundary.**
>
> The game's encounters are derived from documented episodes, circumstances, relationships, projects, movements, writings, possessions, and decisions in John Dee's life. The first vertical slice should form a coherent playable sequence through this material. Player agency exists primarily in how the player understands, prepares for, and executes historically attested choices—not in inventing an alternate career for Dee.

This also changes the meaning of the "Anatomy of an Encounter."

Instead of:

```text
LOCATION + SKILLS + BOOKS + EQUIPMENT
        ↓
generate possible encounter
        ↓
generate choices
```

we want:

```text
DOCUMENTED DEE EPISODE
        ↓
historical situation
        ↓
what Dee knew / possessed / could access
        ↓
people actually involved
        ↓
documented or strongly evidenced actions
        ↓
player decision interface
        ↓
historical outcome
```

The mechanics then determine **how the player reaches and performs the historical decision**, rather than whether the historical episode happens at all.

For example, the Windsor comet episode shouldn't be an encounter generator asking:

> "What might Dee do about a comet?"

It starts with the historical record:

> Dee is confronted with a particular astronomical phenomenon in a particular court context.

The narrative designer then reconstructs the decision environment:

- What did Dee know?
- Which astronomical books did he possess?
- Which instruments were available?
- What mathematical skills could he deploy?
- Who was present?
- What access to court did he have?
- What did the phenomenon mean within the intellectual frameworks available to him?
- What did he actually do?
- What consequences followed?

The player then gets agency **inside that historically bounded situation**.

That also gives us a very useful distinction between three kinds of content:

**Documented action**

Something we have evidence Dee actually did. This should form the backbone of the vertical slice.

**Historically supported interpretation**

The exact decision is not recorded, but the circumstances, capabilities, relationships, or intellectual resources are documented well enough to reconstruct a plausible choice. These can provide limited player agency, but should be identified internally as reconstruction.

**Counterfactual**

Something the player can do that Dee demonstrably did not do, or an encounter involving a relationship/event that did not historically occur. These belong in explicitly marked alternate-history material, not in the core biographical playthrough.

That gives us a much stronger rule for the encounter database:

```text
ENCOUNTER
├── historical_episode
├── date / date_range
├── location
├── participants
├── source_evidence
├── Dee's documented circumstances
├── documented action
├── reconstructed decision space
├── player choices
├── historical outcome
└── consequences
```

And importantly, **the choice tree itself should be evidence-aware**.

A choice isn't justified merely because it is historically plausible. The narrative designer should be able to answer:

> "Why is this a choice Dee could actually have made at this moment?"

That question should become part of the content-authoring workflow.

The vertical slice consequently has a very different success criterion from a conventional RPG:

> **A successful vertical slice lets the player experience a sequence of real Dee episodes while interacting with the books, people, skills, instruments, locations, money, correspondence, and intellectual problems that shaped those episodes.**

The player should come away feeling that they have **played through Dee's life**, not that they have played a generic Renaissance magician who happens to be named John Dee.

And this makes the existing `BOOK + SKILL + CONTACT + PATRON = OPERATION` system much more interesting. Those systems aren't there to generate unlimited procedural adventures. They reconstruct the **historical infrastructure underlying particular episodes**.

For example:

```text
DOCUMENTED EPISODE
        ↓
Dee wants / needs X
        ↓
historical circumstances constrain the problem
        ↓
        ┌──────────────┬───────────────┐
        ↓              ↓               ↓
      BOOK           SKILL          CONTACT
        │              │               │
        └──────────────┼───────────────┘
                       ↓
                 AVAILABLE ACTION
                       ↓
                 HISTORICAL EVENT
```

So the game can have a fairly sophisticated simulation underneath, but the **content pipeline remains biographical**.

That should probably be elevated above "Anatomy of an Encounter" as the first principle of the narrative-design section:

### The Biography Constraint

Every core encounter must originate in Dee's documented biography. The designer begins with a historical episode and reconstructs its playable decision environment. Mechanics determine what the player can know, possess, attempt, and understand within that environment. They do not exist to generate arbitrary new episodes for Dee.

That principle also gives us a clean way to decide what belongs in the first vertical slice: **follow Dee's actual chronology and select a connected chain of documented episodes**, rather than designing six locations and then inventing things for Dee to do at each location.
